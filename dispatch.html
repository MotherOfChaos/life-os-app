🧠Science & Neuroscience
⭐⭐ ADHD + Menopause
ADHD Symptoms and Menopause Are Deeply Linked — And Mostly Invisible to Clinicians
What happened

A King's College London study of 656 women aged 45–60 (245 with ADHD diagnoses) found significant correlations between ADHD symptoms and menopausal complaints — but at a group level these were less prominent in those with ADHD, which could indicate different attribution of symptoms. In plain terms: women with ADHD may be attributing menopausal symptoms to their ADHD (and vice versa) — leading both conditions to be undertreated.

For women who had previously managed their ADHD symptoms well, menopause may initiate a period of increasingly unregulated symptoms. Declining estrogen directly affects the dopaminergic system — the same system at the centre of ADHD neurobiology. This means existing medication doses that worked pre-perimenopause may become insufficient without any change in the ADHD itself.

This is one of the most under-discussed aspects of adult ADHD in women. Perimenopause is not just "getting older" — it is a neurochemical shift that can destabilise a previously managed ADHD profile. The clinical system often misses this entirely. Worth discussing explicitly with any doctor managing both conditions.
⭐ ADHD + Fibromyalgia
ADHD and Fibromyalgia Co-Occur at High Rates — And Each Makes the Other Worse
What happened

A published study found that fibromyalgia patients show significantly higher ADHD symptom scores, and that ADHD scores mediate the relationship between depression severity and quality of life in fibromyalgia patients. The mechanisms likely run in both directions: the central sensitisation underlying fibromyalgia amplifies the cognitive noise of ADHD; and ADHD's dysregulation of the stress response may worsen pain amplification.

Practically: managing ADHD more effectively may reduce fibromyalgia pain — not because they're the same condition, but because they share neurological substrate. ADHD is characterised by a hypodopaminergic state, and people with ADHD are more sensitive to pain than healthy controls.

This explains a pattern many people with both conditions notice: bad ADHD days tend to also be bad pain days. The nervous system isn't compartmentalised — what affects one system affects the whole.
⭐ ADHD Brain
ADHD Brains Slip Into Sleep-Like States While Awake — New Brain Pattern Identified
What happened

Researchers identified a surprising brain pattern that may help explain why people with ADHD often struggle to stay focused: even while awake, their brains can slip into brief episodes of sleep-like activity. These micro-episodes — moments where the brain enters a state normally associated with sleep or near-sleep — last only fractions of a second but are frequent enough to explain the "tuning out" experience that characterises ADHD attention gaps.

This is not laziness or choosing not to pay attention. It is an involuntary neurological event happening in real time, detectable on EEG. The finding has implications for both understanding ADHD and for designing interventions that address the brain state directly, rather than just the behaviour.

Nerve Damage · Cambridge
"Irreversible" Nerve Damage May Not Be — Cambridge Lab Finds Reversal Pathway
What happened

Cambridge researchers created miniature brain-and-spinal-cord systems in the lab that can send signals and trigger muscle contractions. They discovered that human neurons gradually lose their ability to regrow after damage. Critically, they identified the specific point at which this loss occurs — and found that intervening before that point may restore the regeneration capacity. The term "irreversible nerve damage" may need significant qualification for certain injury types and timings.

Relevant for disc hernia outcomes: nerve compression that has been considered permanent may have a treatment window that is longer than currently assumed. A paper worth discussing with any neurologist or neurosurgeon.
Brain Aging · Stanford
Why the Brain Ages: Protein Machinery Begins to Jam — Stanford Discovery
What happened

Scientists at Stanford may have uncovered a hidden reason brains decline with age: the cellular machinery responsible for building proteins begins to jam. Proteins are the workhorses of every cell — they carry out virtually every function. When the machines that build them start to malfunction, errors compound throughout the cell. The study used turquoise killifish, which have extraordinarily short lifespans and rapid aging, allowing the researchers to observe the full arc of brain aging in weeks.

Fatigue · B12
Chronic Fatigue Linked to "Normal" B12 and Folate — But Not Normal Enough
What happened

Researchers in Japan found that low levels of key vitamins — especially vitamin B12 and folate — may quietly contribute to fatigue and lack of motivation, even in apparently healthy people. The study's key finding: levels classified as "within normal range" by standard clinical thresholds may still be insufficient to prevent fatigue, particularly in people with other neurological demands. The optimal B12 level for full neurological function appears to be higher than what most labs flag as deficient.

Relevant for fibromyalgia and ADHD: both conditions carry elevated fatigue burden that can be compounded by sub-optimal B12/folate. Standard blood panels may not catch this — worth requesting an active B12 test (holotranscobalamin) rather than the standard total B12 test.
